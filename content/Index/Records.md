With the LP came the advent of a brand new genre in American music – rock n’ roll. The size of the [[Militarism|military]] from under 2 million active duty personnel in 1941 to over 12 million in 1945, diminishing the number of qualified instrumentalists available to perform. With significantly fewer musicians available, many Big Bands were forced to reduce down to smaller ensembles. In addition to shrinking band sizes, World War II affected the music business on a material level. Many musical instrument manufacturers temporarily converted their factories to produce military contracts, replacing production of new saxophones and pianos with jet engines and ammunition. From 1942 to 1944, the American Federation of Musicians would also impose a strike against the major record labels due to disagreements over royalty payments. Union musicians, including many who performed in Big Bands, were temporarily forbidden from recording new music. Along with vocalists, specialty music genres such as R&B and Country also sold well during the strike. These genres generally relied on smaller rhythm combos and fewer horns than were featured in Big Bands resulting all in all in the arrival of rock n roll.

In the late 70s, Columbia records would witness a huge wave of layoffs for a multitude of reasons and it is speculated the oil shock was one reason for such layoffs. The record industry that was booming in the 60s because of Rock n’ Roll had faced challenges that were then overcome by the cassette tapes that were popularized in the late 70s and the invention of the CD in 1979 which was commercially released in 1982.

The oil shock came to also affect every aspect of the music industry at the time. A [news summary](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/Record-World/70s/73/RW-1973-11-17.pdf) on the vinyl shortage in relation to the music industry by Gary Cohen published on Record World on 1973-11-17 mentions scarcity of vinyl meaning an increase in the cost of manufacturing albums leading to increases in the prices consumers pay for albums. Quality will continue to worsen as manufacturers use recycled vinyl and filler materials. Surface noise, warpage, "clicks and pops" will increase as will customer complaints. Shipping time will increase as plants continue on shorter work weeks, while stores can look forward to being out of stock on hit and catalog albums. Imports, for those stores that carry them, will be harder to get. Radio stations will receive fewer copies of albums. There will be cutbacks on the number of new artists signed, and labels will try to achieve a better spacing of releases. Budget albums are receiving the lowest priority at the pressing level, with classical records close behind. Companies want to use their share of vinyl to press hit albums and achieve the most sales.
