Oil tanker steamships changed navigation as ships were no longer bound to seasons and winds. Marcus Samuel can be credited for the invention of this (founder of Shell, Iraqi-Jew).

The British govt. had backed Samuel and the development of Murex (the first ship) as they felt they could gain an upper hand against the most dominant oil company then, Standard oil. They had feared that Standard would enter Asia (yr. 1898). Within a few years steamships became standard for the shipment of [[Oil]]. 

In the 1830s, the British East India Company began converting its fleet to steamships, signifying the rise of steam technology in maritime transport; however, it took several decades to fully convert all oceangoing ships traveling from the Cape of Good Hope to India. This transition prompted the Government of Bombay to seek a coaling station along the route from Bombay to Suez, leading to the occupation of Soqotra Island in the Indian Ocean in 1835. Unfortunately, Soqotra's harbors were inadequate for shelter, and the island lacked the necessary infrastructure for a coaling station. As a result, the British abandoned Soqotra and, in 1839, forcefully took control of Aden as an alternative strategic location. In the 1840s, the British Admiralty began converting naval vessels to steam power, increasing the demand for imperial coal depots. Between 1850 and 1869, British goods transported by steamships grew substantially from 168,474 to 948,367 net tonnage. Steamship technology and imperial expansion were mutually reinforcing, as imperial steamships required frequent refueling, leading to the conquest of new colonial beachheads along trade routes. These strategic outposts generated additional trade, administrative needs, [[Investments and Trade|capital investment]], labor exploitation, and local knowledge.

The opening of the [[Suez Canal]] in 1869 made British imperial coaling stations and the connected routes even more crucial to Britain's maritime dominance. British primacy over sea routes in the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and the Cape was primarily attributed to coal resources.

As British steam power grew in the late 19th century, Marcus Samuel’s Shell Transport and Trading Company was formed, in 1897, a year before oil was struck in Indonesia and during a time of great angst in the oil markets. In order to secure and propel his position, Samuel wanted to ensure the loyalty of the various trading houses that made the Tank Syndicate in the Far East. The late 19th century was a period were the global demand for oil was rising due to a series of events: crippling economies and famines struck Russia, which caused the local demand for oil to come down and for refiners to export more kerosene into the global market; and in China, Shell’s markets were destroyed along with their facilities due to the Boxer Rebellion; the [[Second Boer-War]] in South Africa also drove prices up; and prior to all of this, his concession was slowly failing. The Royal Dutch faced a similar fate as their oil wells had begun to produce salt water, but after trial and error and with the help of a geologist they were able to produce oil in Sumatra by 1899.
