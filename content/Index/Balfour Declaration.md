The Balfour Declaration was made under the context global economic and military anxieties and of prejudice, faith, and sleight of hand. It is set around the anxieties of the security of Suez Canal and the British military power bases east of the canal, or "Arabia." "Britain’s Balfour policy, historically regressive and historically improbably, was dependent on a highly fortuitous combination of war-related circumstances. Such emphasis on contingency does not preclude the power of motivational forces – these as it were, squeezing through the tight windows of opportunity presented by the first World War, and continuing, with longer-term perspective, to determine appraisals in the year immediately following” (William Matthew). There was no need to annex Palestine for the British influence. The declaration was fixed upon the restoration of "national life" in Palestine. But the war that brought the Zionist commitment was in large part a war between European empires, with the British concerned over the colonies.

The years 1917, 1918, and 1923 should be viewed as different takes on the British mandate. A few example are how the coalition that took over after David Lloyd Georg entertained serious doubts on the value of the declaration, although the 1917 initiatives were imperative for the annexation of Palestine, by the Lloyd Georg Coalition, and in 1920 Lord Peel, and Thomas Haycraft in 1921, took issue with the Zionist presumptions. Sahar Huneidi, in 1998, in her essay “Was the Balfour policy reversible?” makes note on how between the years 1917 to 1922, the British govt. could have reversed the declaration, but they felt as though they needed to honor it still. 
	Another influence was Herbert Sidebotham who was a Zionist journalist.

George was concerned with Palestine as he felt the French would claim it as under the Ottoman rule it was considered to be a part of Syria. But this came from the influence of his younger staff, the “garden suburb:” Leo Amery, Sir Mark Sykes, and Philip Kerr who had served in South Africa under Alfred Milner between 1905-1910, as a member of “Milner’s Kindergarten,” a group of young soi-disant “reformist” imperialist.

The British annexation of Palestine was a defensive military move that predates 1917. The British were moving eastwards of the Suez in 1916. The annexation was not directly correlated with Zionisation. Zionisation was reliant upon the annexation although. The declaration was a territorial formulation, and the continual annexation was fueled by Zionism. After the collapse of the ottoman empire, it felt more urgent to break apart the middle east than to break apart Africa. This was done through several different [[Mandates|mandates]]: 

Class A mandates, the former provinces of Turkey, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Palestine, were considered advanced enough to be given independence, but they were subjected to Allied Adminstrative control “until they were able to stand alone.” The British took Iraq and Palestine, and France took Syria and Lebanon. By 1949, they had all received their independence (Britannica).

Class B mandates included former German-held territories in Africa, “Tanganyika, parts Togoland and the Cameroons, and Ruanda-Urundi.” The allied powers were responsible for administering the mandates but where “intended on protecting the natives.” The British took Tanzania, the French took the Cameroons and Togoland, and Rwanda and Burundi went to Belgium.

Class C mandates, various former German-held territories were transferred to the British: “South West Africa (now Namibia, assigned to South Africa), New Guinea (assigned to Australia), Western Samoa (now Samoa, assigned to New Zealand), the islands north of the Equator in the western Pacific (Japan), Nauru (Australia, with Britain and New Zealand),” and Palestine was suggested to be included in the Class C mandate. _Source Britannica and Further Correspondence Part XVIII – reference FO 414/250_

The League of Nations was responsible supervising these mandates but they did not have a way to enforce the mandates, this system was replaced by the UN trusteeship system in 1946.

Apart of the declarations concern was its access to East Asia, India, and East Africa, similarly, there was a heft amount of discussion of a railway between Palestine and the Persian Gulf. There were threats to the British power from Germany, France, and Russia. Germany had cause alarm in Britain after their project of a Berlin-Baghdad project commenced in 1903, and its eagerness to stir jihadi unrest amongst British Muslim subjects in Asia. 

In 1915-1916 Turkish-German forces were able to cross the Sinai and attack the Suez (with no success). The British came to the conclusion to secure the Suez and India was strategically possession themselves amongst the Hebron mountains and al-Naqab region.

This was brought in war cabinet meetings in 1916-1917, and it was also apart of the British/French partitioning; where the British kept the French strategically away from Egypt.

South African troops who were apart of the [[Second Boer-War]] would be sent to the Sinai. After Louis Botha captured South West Africa from the German, he offered to the British government their army to assist them in the World War, to which they accepted, but the South African troops were oriented and dispatched to the Sinai. 

Apart of the British anxiety was their assets in India, which for them was a powerful military and economic asset for the British. But [[Imperialism|imperialism]] was not a positive confident march, it was an anxious, negative, defensive, and reflexive to threats to its power. British India concerns were reflected across the Mesopotamian, Levantine, and South African regions. The British were ignorant of Palestine. 

They did not know why it was actually important to the British. In a postwar cabinet meeting in 1915 they had considered Egypt and the Suez to territorial options for the British. In 1916, Lord Curzon and Andrew Bonar Law were clear on their imperial motives, and four months prior to the declaration had made the claim that Palestine was important for the British, as it served as a buffer zone for the Suez. There views were shared with (antagonist of the Boer War) Alfred Milner, and Jan Smuts (South African PM). 

Milner described himself as an imperialist out and out. In 1918 to 1921 he had become a member of the War Cabinet, and a colonial secretary and secretary, with a role in preparing the Balfour Declaration. In 1915 he received a copy of the memorandum from Herbet Samuel, and was persuaded in the annexation of Palestine. Alfred Milner had been convinced of a Jewish state since 1914. 

In May of 1917 Smuts had remained in the Imperial War Cabinet which he joined during the war. “Smuts had believed that Palestine was essential on the grounds of strategy and imperial communications. Any other power in control would constitute a serious menace to British imperial communications, as he told Curzon” (cite). Jan Smuts crucial test for Jews was that they should see their movement as naturally and organically linked to British imperial interest. 

While the test for Jews was their pledge to British imperial interest, the test for the British govt. was their pledge to Christian fundamentalism, which they saw as being in debt to or as a supersession of Judaism. “At the time when the Declaration was being drafted, the immediate consideration was to find, as Smuts later put it, ‘a formula to which the Great Powers would agree,’ for staking Britain's claim to the main role in the future of postwar Palestine in cooperation with the Jews” (cite). 

“Palestine, and in particular the southern [sic] highlands, occupied a vital strategic position by the exposed eastern flank of the Canal – what Barbara Tuchman recognizes as its 'fatal geography'. The Manchester Guardian journalist and Zionist supporter Herbert Sidebotham considered this an age-old predicament, Palestine 'in Biblical history' being 'the Belgium of the ancient world', situated as it was between the rival empires of the Nile and Mesopotamia, its possession 'most desirable for any power that is responsible for the defense of Egypt'. Whoever controlled the territory in the twentieth century, the persistent anxiety ran, could gravely compromise British imperial control in India” (William, 237).

Churchill with assume responsibility of Palestine in 1921 and declared in the house of commons:

“Palestine… is all the more important to us ... in view of the ever-growing significance of the Suez Canal; and I do not think 1,000,000 pounds a year ... would be too much to pay for the control and guardianship of this great historic land ...” (Parliamentary Debates, House of Commons, 4 July 1922).

“The conquest of Palestine was the unavoidable precondition for the implementation of the Balfour Declaration. And on the matter of imperialist motivation, we have an impressive roll call: Samuel, Kitchener, Lloyd George, Curzon, Bonar Law, Milner, Smuts, Churchill and the War Office in government; and Amery, Sykes, Kerr and Sidebotham on its influential margins. Some of the remarks may have been casual, tendentious or exaggerated, but the various individuals did all speak or write in unambiguous terms on the critical importance of imperial and strategic considerations, and all in remarkable (if possibly in part recycled) harmony” (William, 243).

By 1922, it becomes clear that there would be no divorce between the marriage of Zionist prejudices and imperial, colonial, settler motivations. That same year Shuckburgh would say that "recent developments have profoundly modified our position in Egypt and circumstances . . . might easily arise, under the new conditions, that would seriously affect our hold upon the Suez Canal. In such a contingency the control of Palestine might be of vital importance to us” (Sir John Shuckburgh, Policy in Palestine, Middle East Dept., 1922). 

In may of 1923 Richard Meinertzhagen, a Zionist veteran of the Egypt and Palestine campaigns, and a British delegate at the 1919 peace conference, was invited to advise the Middle Eastern department of the colonial office on clarifying and assisting the government strategic matters across the Levant. He reiterated the views of previous war cabinet members. When Stanley Badlwin took charge (the new PM) he and his cabinet could not see a new power take control of Palestine as it would be embarrassing. The report was accepted on July 31, 1923. 

In October, Smuts had declared Palestine as an important location:

"Our communications do not seem to be very safe now through Egypt ... We now hold another frontage further in advance in Palestine. I should be very sorry if we had to abandon this front and fall back on the Suez Canal” (At the Imperial Conference, October 1923).

The mandate allowed for the pursuit of pro-zionist policy. It would allow an imperial gain as a self-sustaining European settler community. Herbert Sidebotham was always eager to advance Zionist interest. The direct equation of a Jewish national home with British strategic interests enjoyed wide currency in government circles in the 1910s and after, and Zionists in Britain were eager to feed such metropolitan fantasies. The idea of long term imperial function for the British, through Zionism, had been around for a long time. Zionism was meant to act as a credit for British imperial prospects.

Theodor Herzl, told Lord Lansdowne, then British foreign secretary, that "any concession, anywhere, of colonizing land from the British would mean that 'at one stroke England will get ten million secret but loyal subjects active in all walks of life ... all over the world' , 'ten million agents for her greatness and her influence' . He told Lord (Nathan) Rothschild (father of the Declaration' s recipient, Lionel Walter) that he could expect 'high credit' from the government in London if he was able to help 'strengthen British influence in the Near East by a substantial colonization by our people at the strategic point where Egyptian and Indo-Persian interests converge" (cite).

And like today, Chaim Weizmann believed that a Zionist Palestine would be a natural intermediary between Great Britain and Saudi Arabia.
