The relationship between the [[music]] industry and [[Oil|petroleum]] can be traced back to the beginnings of the oil depletion allowance introduced in 1913 which allowed an oil company to reduce its taxable income by 27 ½ percent (percentage agreed upon in 1926). In January 1938, Roger Marchetti, one of Hollywood’s prominent lawyers, approached Congress to argue that Hollywood and the entertainment industry should receive a similar allowance. Marchetti claimed that a film star only earns a high salary for an average of five to seven years, likening them to oil wells which earn their peak income for several years before facing steep decline. “Popularity is the source of their (film stars) income, and that runs dry as quickly as an oil well does,” he explained. When the attempt failed, Hollywood stars began investing in oil wells in order to turn the oil depletion allowance to their own benefit and to rapidly rising income tax rates in the 1930s.

Bing Crosby (who would participate in an advert for Shell in the 60s called Bing sings Shell) was listed in the 1930s, alongside the estate of the famous oil magnate, John D. Rockefeller (who lived until 1937), as one of the nation’s wealthiest. To shelter a significant part of his income from taxation, Crosby entered into the oil business with his co-star and golfing partner, comedian Bob Hope. They each paid 40,000 USD to Monty Moncrief (a successful Texas oilman as well as their golfing partner) for a 25 percent share in a West Texas venture. This venture earned them $5,000,000 each on their initial 40,000 USD investment. Other Hollywood stars who experienced similar successes in the oil business included Jimmy Stewart, Gene Autry, Don Ameche, and Frank Sinatra, who fittingly titled his first oil well “Crooner No. 1.” in Wyoming according to a [Times article](https://time.com/archive/6603299/oil-the-hollywood-wildcats/) published 1949.

Rock n’ Roll musicians were no strangers to the oil depletion allowance. A piece from 1972 published on [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/knight-vs-funk-an-end-to-brotherhood-117521/3/) mentions the Grand Funk Railroad wanting to avoid paying taxes as not to fund wars thus buying oil wells and starting their oil company White Shield Oil & gas. This decision was supported or perhaps instigated by the band’s manager Terry Knight who had a special interest in energy and had coined the term “enercology” that he defines as "the balanced relationship between energy and ecology and its relative effect on the social environment”. Just before the 1973 [[Oil Embargo (1973)|oil embargo]], Grand Funk Railroad (established 1969) would file a lawsuit represented by Paul Mccarthy lawyer, John Eastman, claiming Knight defrauded them, beguiled them into signing unfair, atypical contracts, and misappropriated their funds. Damages: 8 million USD or so. The lawsuit was settled in 1974 and a [Billboard](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/Billboard/70s/1974/Billboard%201974-03-02.pdf) article that year mentions Terry “will now concentrate most of his time on his extensive oil and mineral holdings among which are interests in White Shield Gas & Oil Corp. that were turned over to him by Grand Funk as part of the settlement”. During that time Terry Knight had been touring universities and speaking on “enercology” since the beginning of the year 1973 according to The Lowell Sun and in response to Nixon's energy crisis address he proposed including “enercology” as a new course of study.
